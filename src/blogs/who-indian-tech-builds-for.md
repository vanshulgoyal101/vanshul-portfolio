Last year I wrote a short post, [Can We Create a Silicon Valley in India?](/blog/silicon-valley-india/), asking whether India should copy the Silicon Valley model or build something from its own materials. I walked through Cyberhub in Gurugram, noticed that everything looked and sounded like an American tech hub, and ended by admitting I didn't have the answer.

I still don't have all of it. But I think I was looking at the wrong layer.

The towers, the cafes, the work culture: those are what you notice first, and they matter least. A tech industry takes the shape of the people who pay it. If you want to know what Indian tech is, the buildings won't tell you much. The invoices will.

## The customer shapes the builder

Silicon Valley's first big customers were close by. The US government bought chips and computers for defense and space programs. Later, American businesses and American consumers bought software. For decades, engineers there spent their working days on the problems of people in their own country, and that piled up into a huge body of knowledge about those problems.

India's software industry grew the other way round. Its first great wave was services: Indian companies building and maintaining software for banks and insurers in America and Europe. It was a real achievement. It built careers and a good part of a new middle class. Many of the most respected Indian product companies later followed a similar path and sold mostly to foreign businesses.

The reason is simple. That's where the paying customers were. Earning in dollars and spending in rupees is one of the best deals an Indian founder can get, and nobody should be blamed for taking it.

But there's a cost that never shows up on a balance sheet. You become what you're paid to understand.

An engineer who spends ten years on the claims process of an American insurer becomes an expert in American insurance. That knowledge is valuable. It's also knowledge about someone else's country. Naval calls this specific knowledge, the kind you can't be trained for and only get by doing the work. India produces a great deal of it every year, and much of it is about the problems of the rich world.

This is the part my first essay missed. I described Gurugram looking American as if it were a style we had borrowed. I now think the offices look like the customer because the work is for the customer. The style follows the invoice.

## Big in people, small in wallets

The obvious reply is: then build for India. It has an enormous number of people. Surely that's the market.

In people, yes. In willingness to pay, it's much smaller, and founders learn this quickly. Indian consumers are famously price-sensitive. Plenty of apps here have reached millions of users and struggled to charge any of them. So the startups that do build for India often end up serving the same thin slice of well-off urban users, which is the part of India that most resembles the rich world anyway.

That leaves a choice that looks like this. Build for foreigners who pay, or build for Indians who mostly don't. Both options pull the best builders away from India's own problems.

I think this framing is wrong in a useful way. India does pay. It just doesn't pay much for software as a thing in itself.

Think about how an ordinary Indian business owner spends. He'll buy a machine or hire more people when he can see how the money comes back. What he resists is a monthly fee for a dashboard. The money exists. It's tied to outcomes, and most software companies are set up to sell tools.

I've felt this from the other side. I know several founders who run large businesses in India, and I've barely used that access. One of the filters I now apply to my own ideas is that the buyer can't build the thing himself and pays fast. It sounds obvious. It also rules out most of what software people in India build by default, because the default buyer is another software person, often abroad.

## Atoms first

There's a deeper mismatch with the template we imported. The Silicon Valley model, as India picked it up, is mostly bits sold to people who already buy bits. Many of India's unsolved problems are atoms first: machines, buildings, boats, roads, with a little software inside. Their buyers are factory owners and cities, and they pay for results. Our best builders have been trained, by every customer they've had, to see the first kind of problem and walk past the second.

Small towns make this plain. I'm from Yamunanagar in Haryana, an industrial town known for its plywood. It is full of businesses that make physical things and real money. Gurugram, where I work, is a few hours' drive away. Yet I struggle to picture a pitch from a typical Gurugram startup that would land with a factory owner there. The owners aren't the problem. The pitch was written for a different customer.

## What a desi YC would have to change

I keep a note about starting an Indian accelerator, a sort of desi YC. It's unscreened. The honest problem written next to it is that I don't know who would pay for it: founders, companies or investors. It also fails my rule that a first version be buildable alone in about ninety days, so any real attempt would have to start far smaller.

Thinking about it through this lens did make one thing clear. Copying YC's format would repeat the mistake of my first essay. Batches and demo days are the surface again. YC works partly because its founders are surrounded by customers who already pay for software. An Indian version would have to do something YC never needed to do. It would have to point founders at Indian buyers who already spend money, and teach them to sell a result to someone who will never buy a tool.

That's harder than running a program. It may be the only version worth running.

## Where I'd look now

Last year I asked whether India should build on an imported template or on its own materials. I now think the material that matters most is the customer. Whoever pays an industry teaches it what to notice. For a generation, India's tech industry has been taught mostly by foreign customers, and it learned well.

The next lesson has to come from home. That means finding the Indians who already pay, in the forms they already pay in, and building for them. The towers in Gurugram would look the same either way. What would change is what the people inside them know.
