Last year I wrote that we live in [two worlds](/blog/the-two-worlds/). In one, people fight for clean water and roads that survive the monsoon. In the other, the basics are invisible and people argue about AGI and Mars. I ended on an image I liked: one world looking at the ground, the other at the stars.

I still think the gap is real. But I now think the picture was wrong in one important way. It assumed both worlds are on the same road, just at different points. First you fix the basics. Then, once the foundation is done, you get to care about everything else.

That order sounds responsible. I've come to think it's one of the things keeping the worlds apart.

## What Kochi looks like from the air

In September I saw Kochi from a plane window. From above, it looks like a large village growing out of a palm-tree forest. On the water, the fishing and boating industry still runs the traditional way: old wooden boats, old nets, rough embankments.

The note I wrote down afterwards was one line. Having beautiful cities is not a luxury. It is a necessity in the 21st century.

I want to be careful about what that means. I'm not saying Kochi should turn into Miami or San Francisco overnight. I mean that a city should at least be trying to look better, and that most cities like it aren't, because everyone has quietly agreed that beauty comes later.

## Why beauty gets cut first

It's easy to see how that happens. When money is short, you count things. Kilometres of road. Homes with a water connection. Megawatts installed. These numbers are honest and they matter.

But beauty doesn't show up in any of them. Neither does quality. A road and a well-built road count the same on the day they open.

Whatever doesn't get counted gets cut. A drain goes in without a cover. An embankment is concrete poured onto mud. A bus stop is a sheet of metal on two poles. Each choice is defensible on its own. Together they make a place nobody is proud of.

And places nobody is proud of don't get looked after. Something that looks temporary gets treated as temporary. Litter collects where there is already litter. Cheap work that has to be redone every few years ends up costing more than good work done once. The basic version is often not the cheap version. It's the version whose costs arrive later, in someone else's budget.

## The people who can leave

There's a second cost, and I think it's the bigger one.

For most of the last century, cities competed for factories. Factories go where land, power and labour are cheap. A factory doesn't care if the town is ugly.

Now a lot of the most valuable work can be done from almost anywhere: software, design, research, the work that builds the other world. The people who do it are some of the most mobile people alive. They choose where to live the way anyone would. They pick somewhere clean, calm and pleasant to walk around in.

So a city that postpones beauty until the basics are finished is quietly exporting the people who could have finished them. They leave for Bangalore, then maybe for London or San Francisco. They send money home, which helps. But the companies they start, the taxes those companies pay and the people they train all land somewhere else.

This is the mechanism I missed. The two worlds don't just sit at different stages. The gap pulls people across it, in one direction. Beauty is part of how a place keeps the people who would fix its basics.

Kochi's water shows the other side of this. The same backwaters that carry old wooden boats could carry better boats, along cleaner embankments, and open up to higher-value uses like yachts, speedboats and water sports. That isn't decoration. It's a richer economy sitting on the same water, waiting for someone to treat the waterfront as an asset rather than a utility.

## Skipping the middle

The second thing I got wrong was about technology. I wrote that it can bridge the gap but often widens it, because the two worlds use the same tools for different games.

What I underrated is that the newest technology is often the cheapest way to get the basics. India didn't finish laying landlines before most people got a phone. Most people simply skipped the landline.

Electricity may be going the same way. I work on the website for a rooftop solar business in Punjab and Haryana, and through it I've seen how ordinary the product has become. Homes and small businesses put panels on their own roofs, some tied to the grid, some with batteries, with help from a government subsidy scheme. The panels are the easy part now. The slow part is the process with the local electricity company: a feasibility check, an approval, an agreement, a new meter.

That's a small detail, but it says something. The technology arrived already cheap. What slows it down is the old system around it. A place with a weak grid and a lot of sun doesn't have to build a twentieth-century grid first. It can go straight to power made on the roof, if its institutions let it.

AI might do the same for expertise. The basics world isn't only short of roads. It's short of agronomists, doctors, engineers and teachers, and it will stay short as long as each one has to be trained individually and then persuaded not to leave. A model on a cheap phone won't replace a good doctor. But it can be a first opinion for someone who currently has none.

I got a small taste of this through the NASA Space Apps Collective. I worked with an international team on weather visualisations for farming communities in Zimbabwe, built from open NASA data on soil, precipitation, temperature and humidity. The aim was to help with crop-rotation planning. I can't tell you it changed a harvest; we never measured that. But it did break my own metaphor. That data comes from satellites, about as "stars" as anything gets, and its most obvious use was the ground.

## Development is not a ladder

Put these together and "basics first" looks like a trap with two halves.

The first half is about quality. Treat beauty as a reward for finishing the basics, and you build places nobody maintains and nobody stays in, so the basics never quite finish.

The second half is about technology. Treat the frontier as something for richer countries, and you end up buying the previous generation because it feels more appropriate. You keep the wooden boat because the better one seems like a luxury.

Both come from the same assumption: that development is a ladder you climb one rung at a time. Some rungs can now be skipped. And some things that look like the top rung, like a beautiful waterfront or a tool built at the frontier, turn out to be holding the ladder up.

I don't think the gap closes by itself. But I no longer think the poorer world's job is to finish the foundation and only then look up. A better question for a city like Kochi is what it would build if it assumed it would still be here, and proud of itself, in fifty years. The answer usually costs a bit more up front. It's still cheaper than building the same cheap thing three times while the people who could have built it well move away.
